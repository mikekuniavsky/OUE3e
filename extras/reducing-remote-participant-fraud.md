# Reducing remote participant fraud

From [Chapter 8, Usability Testing]({{ '/chapters/usability-testing/' | relative_url }}). Chapter 4 on [Recruiting]({{ '/chapters/recruiting/' | relative_url }}) covers screeners and incentives.
{: .fs-5 }

Here are some guidelines to reduce remote participant fraud. Thanks to the University of Toronto's Centre for Research & Innovation Support and its online guide on this topic.

- **Assume there will be some fraud.** Budget for recruiting additional participants on the assumption that you'll need to throw out the data from 25% of them.
- **Don't automatically pay incentive payments.** Pay participants manually, only after reviewing their usability tests. Budget for the extra time for someone to review them.
- **Require a multi-stage recruitment and screening process,** perhaps with a quick video chat interview.
- **Include highly specific screening questions** that can identify whether someone is or is not part of your target audience. Preferred ones have answers that are not easily guessed. For example, for an app for SF Bay Area food truck owners: "If your food truck parks in 3 cities to catch the lunchtime rush in San Francisco, the peninsula, and the East Bay, how many permits does it need to legally operate?" (The answer: at least 6!) That's extreme, but if you really want to make sure you get food truck operators, that'll filter out the wannabes.
- **Use open-ended questions and evaluate the answers,** looking out for AI-generated replies.
- **Phrase similar questions in different ways** to verify that the answers match.
- **Note how long it took for someone to complete the usability test.** If it takes people twelve minutes when you do a pre-test with colleagues, a random participant shouldn't be able to do it in five minutes.
- **Restrict participation to people with Single Sign On IDs** (such as Google, LinkedIn or Facebook). This can verify that the person is a real person and only permits one test per account. The downside is that, as a purely technical solution, someone has to implement it.
- **Work with pre-vetted participant panels,** such as those available through many of the remote usability testing platforms. The advantage is that you can be pretty sure there will not be quite as much fraud as with other methods. The disadvantage is that the people on these panels often do many usability tests and are essentially professional usability testers, which limits the quality of their responses.
- **Require participants to have their cameras turned on** and to allow the test to be recorded. This may not be possible in all situations.
- **Use separate systems for screening and for the research itself.** The University of Toronto reports that there are many bots that jump directly from the screening to the end of the research to collect an incentive.
- **Geographically limit both your recruiting and your incentive.** The internet is international, but your research may be more relevant to people in one region than another. If you're testing a product for a US-based healthcare organization, the response of someone in Italy will not be helpful. Incentive payments may be geo-locked when using virtual credit cards (though, like everything, people can circumvent this, it's at least a speed bump).
